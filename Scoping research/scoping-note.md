# Scoping note: five papers coauthored by Andrew Golightly

The five PDFs in `Source papers` are a single research programme. Each one does Bayesian inference for a stochastic dynamic model whose observed-data likelihood cannot be evaluated directly: a Markov jump process, a chemical Langevin equation or other stochastic differential equation, or a stochastic epidemic compartment model, observed discretely and often only in part. A particle filter, or a related simulator, can estimate that likelihood or propose data-consistent paths. Naive simulation is either too expensive, too fragile when an observation can kill every particle, or wasteful when a cheap surrogate is available. The surrogates that recur are the linear noise approximation, a conditioned bridge, and the ensemble Kalman filter.

Read in chronological order, the papers move from accelerating an exact pseudo-marginal sampler, to approximate Bayesian computation and sequential epidemic fitting, and then to high-dimensional filtering and a bounded alive particle filter.

## Summaries

### Accelerating inference for stochastic kinetic models

Lowe, Golightly and Sherlock (2023). *Computational Statistics and Data Analysis* 185, 107760. [10.1016/j.csda.2023.107760](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csda.2023.107760). Source file: `38253VoR.pdf`.

Stochastic kinetic models, as Markov jump processes or as chemical Langevin equations, have intractable likelihoods once species are observed discretely, partially, and with error. The paper keeps particle Markov chain Monte Carlo, so the chain still targets the exact posterior, and uses the linear noise approximation in three places at once: a Metropolis-adjusted Langevin proposal built from the surrogate gradient, a residual bridge that guides particles between observations, and the cheap first stage of a delayed-acceptance step. Correlated particle filters supply a further variance reduction. On an epidemic model, an aphid growth model, and Lotka–Volterra, the bridge does most of the work. Solving the linear-noise ODEs once per iteration, rather than once per particle, gives a small extra saving. Langevin proposals raised overall efficiency by about an order of magnitude on a three-parameter target.

### Towards data-conditional simulation for ABC inference in stochastic differential equations

Jovanovski, Golightly and Picchini (2024). *Bayesian Analysis*. [10.1214/24-BA1467](https://doi.org/10.1214/24-BA1467). Source file: `3033124AOV.pdf`.

Discretely observed stochastic differential equations rarely have a closed-form transition density, so the paper works with approximate Bayesian computation rather than an unbiased likelihood estimator. The sampler is sequential Monte Carlo ABC, but proposed trajectories come from a lookahead particle filter followed by backward simulation, so simulated paths stay consistent with the observations and the acceptance rate rises. Summary statistics are not fixed in advance: a partially exchangeable network, built for Markov processes, is retrained after each round. Five simulation studies cover three members of the Chan–Karaolyi–Longstaff–Sanders family, the bistable Schlögl model, and a two-dimensional biochemical network. Against forward-only ABC-SMC the scheme is about three times faster, and about ten times faster in some of the harder cases. Most of that gain arrives in the first few rounds, when the data-conditional proposals pull the population into the bulk of the posterior.

### Sequential Bayesian inference for stochastic epidemic models of cumulative incidence

Whitaker, Golightly, Gillespie and Kypraios (2025). *Bayesian Analysis*. [10.1214/25-BA1547](https://doi.org/10.1214/25-BA1547). Source file: `4992336VoR.pdf`.

Fitting a continuous-time SEIR jump process by data-augmented MCMC does not scale past a few thousand cases, and incidence data are incomplete. The paper replaces the jump process by a discrete-time model, dS(E)IR, in which event counts over a user-chosen step are Poisson, and time-varying contact and reporting rates enter through discretised stochastic differential equations. Conditional on the latent cumulative incidence, the static parameters have a low-dimensional sufficient statistic and a tractable conditional posterior. A particle filter therefore propagates incidence trajectories, resamples them, and rejuvenates parameters by direct simulation from that conditional posterior, in the spirit of Storvik and of particle learning. Trajectories are pushed towards the next incidence count by a conditioned hazard. On synthetic SIR data the filter matches long pseudo-marginal runs once it has enough particles, and a parallel implementation gives a large speed-up. On Ebola in West Africa it forecasts the end of the epidemic more faithfully than a linear noise approximation when counts are low. On COVID-19 in New York its uncertainty is tighter than that of an ODE transmission model with a stochastic infection rate. A new observation is assimilated in about six minutes for dSEIR and about two minutes for dSIR.

### Nested ensemble Kalman filter for static parameter inference in nonlinear state-space models

Golightly, Heaps, Sherlock, Wadkin and Wilkinson (2026). *Statistics and Computing* 36, 149. [10.1007/s11222-026-10899-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11222-026-10899-5). Source file: `5417950VOR.pdf`. Code: [github.com/AndyGolightly/NestedEnKF](https://github.com/AndyGolightly/NestedEnKF).

SMC² estimates static parameters sequentially by weighting parameter particles with a particle-filter likelihood and rejuvenating them with pseudo-marginal Metropolis–Hastings. That inner filter becomes impractical as the observation dimension grows. The nested ensemble Kalman filter keeps the SMC² skeleton and replaces the state particle filter with an ensemble Kalman filter, so parameter particles are weighted and mutated under the ensemble Kalman likelihood. A k-nearest-neighbour approximation of that likelihood screens proposals inside a delayed-acceptance rejuvenation step. For nonlinear or non-Gaussian observations, where the ensemble Kalman filter itself is a biased update, a Rao–Blackwellised particle filter uses the ensemble Kalman filter only to propagate states and to approximate the transition density, then corrects with an importance weight. The scheme is plug-and-play: it needs forward simulation and an observation density. Four examples of increasing dimension are an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, Lotka–Volterra with a nonlinear observation model, the spread of oak processionary moth in south-east England, and a ten-dimensional Lorenz-96 system. The method is aimed at problems with few static parameters and many observed components.

### Robust, partially alive particle Metropolis–Hastings via the Frankenfilter

Sherlock, Golightly and Lee (2026). *Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics*. [10.1080/10618600.2026.2732158](https://doi.org/10.1080/10618600.2026.2732158). Source file: `5836428AAM.pdf`.

If the conditional likelihood of an observation can be exactly zero, as with noiseless observations of a Markov jump process or an approximate Bayesian computation kernel, a fixed-size particle filter can return a zero likelihood estimate and the alive particle filter can run for an unbounded time before it records enough successes. The Frankenfilter targets a chosen amount of success on each inter-observation interval and places explicit lower and upper bounds on the number of simulations. The resulting likelihood estimator stays unbiased, so pseudo-marginal Metropolis–Hastings remains valid. For a run of exact observations the tuning rule is to target as many successes as there are observations, and to set the upper simulation bound so that it is hit on a small but non-negligible fraction of intervals. Studies on a pure death process, protein dimerisation, Lotka–Volterra under tau-leaping and under an SDE, and chronic wasting disease in mule deer show greater robustness to outliers and to poor initial parameter values than a standard particle filter, with efficiency typically at least two to three times higher and, in some cases, more than ten times higher. An alive filter with a hard threshold remains biased even when that threshold is large.

## Future directions

The directions below are the ones the papers themselves leave open. A short list of joins across the five papers follows.

### From the kinetic-model paper (Lowe, Golightly and Sherlock, 2023)

- Map when Langevin proposals keep their advantage as the number of rate constants grows. Mixing should improve with dimension, while the cost of the surrogate ODEs grows linearly in the number of rates.
- Decide when a simplified gradient, which ignores the dependence of the linear-noise variance on the parameters, is accurate enough. This matters most when only a few particles are required.
- Try Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the full surrogate gradient on the same partial-observation problems. The simplified gradient is a poor candidate: dropping the variance dependence means the potential energy is not conserved, so acceptance should suffer even at a small step size. Stathopoulos and Girolami (2013) already used Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the linear noise approximation as the inferential model itself, in a sacrificial-observation setting.

### From the ABC paper (Jovanovski, Golightly and Picchini, 2024)

- Run a hybrid sampler: data-conditional simulation for the first few rounds, then switch to ordinary forward simulation once the population sits near the posterior. Forward simulation is the cheaper proposal once the approximation is already close, and each extra round also pays to retrain the summary network.
- Use a higher-order discretisation for the forward pass, and Euler–Maruyama or Milstein transition densities only to weight the backward pass. The two densities will not match, but the backward step then selects among accurate forward paths. Buckwar, Tamborrino and Tubikanec (2020) show that Euler–Maruyama can distort the ABC posterior for some diffusions.
- Extend automatic summaries beyond one dimension. The partially exchangeable network used here applies to one-dimensional diffusions. The backward-simulation ABC-SMC step itself already applies in several dimensions if summaries are built another way. Summaries that estimate the posterior mean, as in Fearnhead and Prangle (2012) and in the neural approaches of Jiang et al. (2017) and Akesson et al. (2021), keep the synthetic likelihood on safer ground.
- Expect larger speed-ups on SDEs whose paths are more erratic than the cases already tried. The biggest gains in the paper already come from the harder examples.

### From the epidemic paper (Whitaker, Golightly, Gillespie and Kypraios, 2025)

- Replace the fixed Poisson step with an adaptive step. Accuracy relative to the underlying jump process, and the cost of the filter, both depend on the phase of the epidemic. Gillespie (2001) gives conditions for the Poisson approximation. Cao, Gillespie and Petzold (2006) and Sandmann (2009) give adaptive tau-leaping.
- Rebuild the conditioned hazard for epidemics observed so sparsely that cumulative incidence is not approximately linear between observations. The current hazard comes from a Gaussian approximation plus that linearity assumption, and it will propose inconsistent trajectories when the assumption fails. Guided samplers for conditioned jump processes, in particular Golightly and Sherlock (2019) and Corstanje, van der Meulen and Schauer (2023), are the suggested route.
- Take the filter to longer time series. The sufficient statistic degenerates in the same way particle learning degenerates: the number of distinct ancestral values feeding the statistic falls exponentially in time (Chopin et al., 2010). The applications here stay short enough, and use enough particles, to avoid the worst of this. A direct comparison with resample-move schemes such as SMC² (Chopin, Jacob and Papaspiliopoulos, 2013) is left open.

### From the nested ensemble Kalman filter paper (Golightly et al., 2026)

- Replace the linear nearest-neighbour lookup in the delayed-acceptance screen with a KD-tree once the parameter dimension is moderate. Lookup then scales like the dimension times the log of the number of retained particles, rather than linearly in that number. Sherlock, Golightly and Henderson (2017) is the cited construction.
- Choose, inside a run, among delayed-acceptance kernels with different neighbour counts and a plain Metropolis–Hastings kernel. Early in the filter the likelihood estimator is cheap, so screening buys less. Fearnhead and Taylor (2013) and Botha, Kohn, South and Drovandi (2023) have used related switching ideas in iterated batch importance sampling and in SMC².
- Put a gradient-based proposal inside the resample-move step (Rosato et al., 2024).
- Use the nearest-neighbour surrogate for weighting as well as for mutation, at a fixed ensemble size. That gives a fixed computational cost and a less accurate posterior.
- Treat the method as the tool for a specific regime: few static parameters relative to the number of observed series. The discussion gives an illustrative scale of fewer than about ten parameters and more than about ten observed components.

### From the Frankenfilter paper (Sherlock, Golightly and Lee, 2026)

- Compare success functions. The filter is valid for any success function. This paper uses a normalised conditional likelihood. A constant success function reduces the method to an ordinary particle filter. Other choices may do better than the one used in the studies.
- Test the tuning rules outside the cases covered by the proofs. One result is for full-vector exact observations, one for partial exact observations, and two require the success function to be proportional to the weight. The practical advice, target about as many successes as there are observation times and hit the upper simulation bound only occasionally, looks reasonable in the studies and still needs a wider check.
- Account for dependence across intervals. With a tight upper bound the Frankenfilter controls how hard a single interval is to simulate, and the likelihood-estimator variance can still grow because average weights on successive intervals are positively correlated.
- Prove a central limit theorem for the likelihood estimator in the regime where both the success target and the hard upper bound diverge. Le Gland and Oudjane (2006) give a central limit theorem for a closely related, slightly biased estimator with no hard threshold. Transferring that argument is left open because of technical details in the thresholded case. Non-asymptotic relative-variance bounds, which matter more directly for pseudo-marginal Metropolis–Hastings, are already given.

### Directions that join the five papers

These are not claims made in a single discussion section. They are the open problems that appear once the five papers are read together.

- The same surrogate is sometimes a proposal or a screen, and sometimes the likelihood itself. The kinetic-model paper and the Frankenfilter correct the surrogate and target the exact posterior. The epidemic paper and the ABC paper change the model or the target. The nested ensemble Kalman filter sits in between: the plain version targets the posterior under the ensemble Kalman likelihood, and the Rao–Blackwellised version puts a correction back in the weight. A useful piece of theory is a rule for when that correction is worth its cost.
- Conditioned simulation is the common device for informative data: residual bridges, lookahead and backward simulation, conditioned hazards, and the alive filter's insistence on successful particles. Every paper that uses a linear-Gaussian guide also warns that the guide fails when the interval is long or the dynamics are strongly nonlinear. That is the same gap the epidemic paper names for sparse incidence and the ABC paper names for erratic diffusion paths.
- Batch pseudo-marginal samplers (the kinetic-model paper and the Frankenfilter) and sequential filters (the epidemic paper and the nested ensemble Kalman filter) are answers to different operating regimes. The epidemic paper asks for an SMC² comparison on long series. The nested ensemble Kalman filter is one such comparison, aimed at high-dimensional observations. Running that comparison on the Ebola and COVID-19 incidence series would close a loop the 2025 paper leaves open.
- Exact zeros and ABC kernels are the same computational event: the conditional likelihood is an indicator. The Frankenfilter introduction already treats an ABC ball as a source of zero likelihoods. Using a bounded alive filter as the inner simulator in data-conditional ABC would join the 2024 and 2026 papers.
- Gradient information is already computed from the linear noise approximation in the 2023 paper, and the 2026 paper asks for gradient-based resample-move proposals. Carrying the linear-noise gradient into the nested ensemble Kalman filter, or into the ABC-SMC mutation kernel, is a direct transfer.
- Real-time epidemic fitting needs a cheap update per new observation. The 2025 filter gives minute-scale updates on short series and will degenerate on long ones. High-dimensional observations and intervals on which every particle dies are the two failure modes addressed by the nested ensemble Kalman filter and the Frankenfilter.

## Literature worth reading next

The list is short on purpose. It is the work these five papers rest on, plus the items their own future-work paragraphs point to. Golightly's earlier papers are listed first because the five PDFs treat them as known.

### His earlier papers that these five build on

- Golightly, A. and Wilkinson, D. J. (2011). Bayesian parameter inference for stochastic biochemical network models using particle Markov chain Monte Carlo. *Interface Focus* 1, 807–820. An early application of particle MCMC to biochemical networks, and the starting point for the later bridge and surrogate papers.
- Golightly, A. and Wilkinson, D. J. (2015). Bayesian inference for Markov jump processes with informative observations. *Statistical Applications in Genetics and Molecular Biology* 14, 169–188. Introduces the conditioned hazard used for incidence bridges and for guided jump-process proposals.
- Golightly, A., Henderson, D. A. and Sherlock, C. (2015). Delayed acceptance particle MCMC for exact inference in stochastic kinetic models. *Statistics and Computing* 25, 1039–1055. The delayed-acceptance particle MCMC scheme that the 2023 paper combines with bridges and Langevin proposals, and that the 2026 paper cites for its rejuvenation screen.
- Whitaker, G. A., Golightly, A., Boys, R. J. and Sherlock, C. (2017). Improved bridge constructs for stochastic differential equations. *Statistics and Computing* 27, 885–900. The diffusion-bridge constructions extended by the residual bridges in the 2023 paper.
- Golightly, A. and Kypraios, T. (2018). Efficient SMC² schemes for stochastic kinetic models. *Statistics and Computing* 28, 1215–1230. Sequential Monte Carlo for the same model class, and the natural comparison point for the epidemic filter.
- Golightly, A. and Sherlock, C. (2019). Efficient sampling of conditioned Markov jump processes. *Statistics and Computing* 29, 1149–1163. The bridge paper cited for sparse epidemics, and an empirical check of the "successes ? number of observations" rule (seven observation times, eight successes).
- Golightly, A., Bradley, E., Lowe, T. and Gillespie, C. S. (2019). Correlated pseudo-marginal schemes for time-discretised stochastic kinetic models. *Computational Statistics and Data Analysis* 136, 92–107. Correlation of the random numbers inside the particle filter, reused in the 2023 sampler.
- Golightly, A., Wadkin, L. E., Whitaker, S. A., Baggaley, A. W., Parker, N. G. and Kypraios, T. (2023). Accelerating Bayesian inference for stochastic epidemic models using incidence data. *Statistics and Computing* 33, 134. The immediate predecessor of the 2025 incidence filter.
- Drovandi, C., Everitt, R. G., Golightly, A. and Prangle, D. (2022). Ensemble MCMC: accelerating pseudo-marginal MCMC for state space models using the ensemble Kalman filter. *Bayesian Analysis* 17, 223–260. Offline ensemble MCMC. The nested ensemble Kalman filter is the sequential counterpart, and one rejuvenation step is one iteration of this scheme.

- Fearnhead, P. and Taylor, B. M. (2013). An adaptive sequential Monte Carlo sampler. *Bayesian Analysis* 8, 411–438. Adaptive sequential Monte Carlo, cited as a precedent for switching kernels inside the nested filter.
- Botha, I., Kohn, R., South, L. and Drovandi, C. (2023). Automatically adapting the number of state particles in SMC². *Statistics and Computing* 33, 82. Their companion preprint, Adaptively switching between a particle marginal Metropolis–Hastings and a particle Gibbs kernel in SMC² (arXiv:2307.11553), is the other adaptive-kernel precedent.
- Sherlock, C., Golightly, A. and Henderson, D. A. (2017). Adaptive, delayed-acceptance MCMC for targets with expensive likelihoods. *Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics* 26, 434–444. The KD-tree nearest-neighbour screen suggested as a faster delayed-acceptance surrogate.

## A compact reading order

1. Wilkinson (2018), chapters on jump processes, the chemical Langevin equation, and the linear noise approximation, plus Andrieu, Doucet and Holenstein (2010).
2. Golightly and Wilkinson (2015), Golightly and Sherlock (2019), and Fearnhead, Giagos and Sherlock (2014). These three make the bridges and the surrogate in the 2023 paper intelligible.
3. The 2023 kinetic-model paper, then the 2024 ABC paper. Read Fearnhead and Prangle (2012) alongside the ABC paper.
4. Chopin, Jacob and Papaspiliopoulos (2013), then the 2025 epidemic paper and the 2026 nested ensemble Kalman filter.
5. Del Moral et al. (2015) and Sherlock et al. (2015), then the Frankenfilter.

That order follows the dependence among the five papers, and it puts the two 2026 papers after the sequential Monte Carlo background they assume.
