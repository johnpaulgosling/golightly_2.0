## Chopin, Jacob and Papaspiliopoulos

- Citation, as printed: N. Chopin, P. E. Jacob and O. Papaspiliopoulos, "SMC2: an efficient algorithm for sequential analysis of state-space models", arXiv:1101.1528v3, 27 January 2012. The file is a preprint. Later papers in this folder cite the journal version, *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series B* 75, 397-426 (2013). That journal version was not the file read here.
- Path: `Literature/1101.1528v3.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: how to explore the sequence of parameter posteriors \(\pi_t(\theta \mid y_{1:t})\) in a state-space model when the likelihood increments are intractable but can be estimated without bias by a particle filter.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the incremental weight is an unbiased estimator of the likelihood increment (Section 3.1, display (7) and Proposition 1). The ensemble Kalman likelihood (13) in Golightly et al. (2026) is not that estimator. The rejuvenation kernel is particle MCMC on the extended target that includes the particle-filter variables. There is no ensemble, no nearest-neighbour screen, and no surrogate weight.
- Relevance to E1: not relevant. The paper does not discuss nearest neighbours or trees.
- Relevance to E2: Section 3.2 shows that a Metropolis-Hastings kernel on the extended target, with the unbiased likelihood estimator inside the acceptance ratio, leaves that target invariant. Fearnhead and Taylor are not the source of this argument. The invariance uses unbiasedness. It does not by itself justify switching kernels when the weight is an ensemble Kalman likelihood or a nearest-neighbour surrogate. Section 3.6 discusses changing the number of state particles, which is a different adaptation from E2.
- Relevance to E3: not relevant. Proposals in Section 3.2 are a general kernel \(T(\theta, \mathrm{d}\tilde{\theta})\). No gradient of a filter is derived.
- Relevance to E4: the exactness argument is a constraint. Proposition 1 (Section 3.1) obtains the exact parameter posterior as a marginal only because the product of incremental weights is unbiased for the true likelihood. Replacing that product by a nearest-neighbour prediction removes the step that identifies the marginal. The paper does not give a substitute limit for a biased weight.
- Relevance to E5: Section 1.2 recalls that the variance of the particle-filter likelihood estimator increases linearly in time, citing Cérou et al. (2011), which was not opened. Section 3.7 discusses computational cost. The file does not report a grid of parameter dimension against observation dimension.
- Relevance to E6: not relevant. There is one target, the exact posterior, and no Rao-Blackwellised ensemble correction.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: the SMC2 recursion in Section 3; Proposition 1 in Section 3.1, which identifies the extended target and its \(\theta\)-marginal; the particle Metropolis-Hastings kernel in Section 3.2, whose invariant distribution is that extended target. Sections 4 and 5 were read for the model classes (Lévy-driven stochastic volatility, athletic records) and for the claim that the algorithm estimates the evidence as a by-product. The numerical tables in Section 4 were not transcribed, and the supplement mentioned in Section 1.3 is not in the file.
- What the paper does not give us: a justification for a biased likelihood, a cost comparison with an ensemble Kalman filter, or a delayed-acceptance screen.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 filter keeps this outer recursion and replaces the inner particle filter, and with it the unbiasedness that Proposition 1 uses.

## Sherlock, Golightly and Henderson

- Citation, as printed: Chris Sherlock, Andrew Golightly and Daniel A. Henderson, "Adaptive, delayed-acceptance MCMC for targets with expensive likelihoods", arXiv:1509.00172v2, 1 June 2016. The research plan identifies the published version as Sherlock, Golightly and Henderson (2017), *Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics*. That journal file was not read.
- Path: `Literature/1509.00172v2.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: how to screen Metropolis-Hastings proposals with an inverse-distance nearest-neighbour surrogate, stored in a KD-tree that grows during the run, while still targeting the posterior that the expensive likelihood defines.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the construction is batch MCMC, not SMC2. Theorem 1 (Section 3.5) is ergodicity of an adaptive mixture of a fixed kernel and a delayed-acceptance kernel. For the pseudo-marginal version, Assumption 2 requires the importance weights to be uniformly bounded, and the second stage uses an unbiased estimator of the posterior (Section 3.4, display (6)). The ensemble Kalman likelihood is not unbiased for the true likelihood. The paper's tree stores evaluations of the true posterior or of that unbiased estimator, not of a biased ensemble Kalman likelihood, and it is not rebuilt inside a resample-move step at every time \(t\).
- Relevance to E1: this is the construction Section 6 of Golightly et al. (2026) cites. Section 2 states that a balanced tree searches and inserts in \(O(d \log n)\) operations, against \(O(n)\) for a list, for moderate \(d\) (their applications use dimensions 3 to 12). Section 2.1 warns that axis-aligned splits are inefficient when components of \(\theta\) are strongly correlated, and recommends a preliminary standardisation. Section 2.7 and the leaf-median rule are about keeping the tree balanced as it grows. The paper does not claim that an approximate neighbour search leaves the same kernel as an exact search.
- Relevance to E2: Algorithm 1 (Section 3.2) already mixes a fixed Metropolis-Hastings kernel, chosen with probability \(\beta\), and a delayed-acceptance kernel. Theorem 2, stated in Section 3.5 and proved in the supplement, says that if \(\beta\) is proportional to the stage-one acceptance rate, the number of iterations should be increased so that the expected number of expensive evaluations stays constant. That is a batch-MCMC rule. It is not a rule for choosing \(k\) inside SMC2, and it assumes the second stage evaluates the intended target.
- Relevance to E3: not relevant. Proposals are random walks.
- Relevance to E4: Section 5.1 is a constraint. The authors write that their algorithm overlays a standard Metropolis-Hastings or pseudo-marginal kernel, so the expensive posterior is evaluated at every accepted point, and that using the surrogate as the target would invalidate the draws. They contrast this with methods that target the approximation. E4 does the second thing. The paper does not prove what an SMC algorithm with surrogate weights converges to.
- Relevance to E5: the cost statement in Section 2 is for moderate \(d\), and the authors say a KD-tree is the wrong structure for very low dimension (a quadtree analogue) and that correlation across coordinates degrades axis-aligned splits. They do not study observation dimension.
- Relevance to E6: not relevant.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: the two-stage acceptance probabilities (2) and (3) in Section 3.1, whose product satisfies detailed balance for the expensive target; the pseudo-marginal stage-two probability (9) in Section 3.4; Theorem 1 in Section 3.5; the tree operations in Section 2. The supplement's proof of Theorem 1 was not read. The simulation claims in Section 5 (factors of about 7 and 4 on two examples) were read as discussion text, not recomputed.
- What the paper does not give us: an SMC2 implementation, a treatment of a biased ensemble Kalman likelihood inside the tree, or a guarantee for approximate rather than exact neighbours.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 screen uses the same inverse-distance average, but the neighbour search in the applications is a list, and the target of the second stage is the ensemble Kalman posterior rather than the exact posterior.

## Botha, Kohn, South and Drovandi

- Citation, as printed: Imke Botha, Robert Kohn, Leah South and Christopher Drovandi, "Automatically adapting the number of state particles in SMC2", arXiv:2201.11354v2, 21 October 2022 (the title page also prints 24 October 2022).
- Path: `Literature/2201.11354v2.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: how to choose the number of state particles \(N_x\) inside SMC2, and how to refresh the state particles after that change, without leaving the exact parameter posterior.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the likelihood estimator is the unbiased particle-filter estimator, and for any fixed \(N_x\) the algorithm targets the exact posterior (Introduction, citing Duan and Fulop, 2014). The authors explicitly set ensemble MCMC aside because it is approximate (Introduction). The adaptation is of \(N_x\) and of the number of MCMC iterations inside the mutation, not of the choice among delayed-acceptance kernels. The companion preprint on switching between particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings and particle Gibbs is not this file.
- Relevance to E1: not relevant.
- Relevance to E2: a constraint, and not a solution. Section 4.1 triggers adaptation when the expected squared jumping distance of the mutation falls below a target, and then adapts \(N_x\) and the number of mutation iterations. Section 6 reports that the rule based on expected squared jumping distance was the most consistent of the rules they tried, on the models in Section 5. The 2026 discussion cites "Botha et al. (2023a)" for choosing among kernels. This preprint is the particle-count paper, and its Section 6 does not contain that choice. An invariance argument taken from this file uses an unbiased estimator and a mutation kernel that preserves the exact extended target. It does not transfer to an ensemble Kalman weight without a fresh argument.
- Relevance to E3: not relevant. No filter gradient is computed.
- Relevance to E4: the same exactness constraint as in Chopin et al. Section 4.3's `replace` update is designed so that changing \(N_x\) does not change the parameter weights. That is the opposite of E4, which changes the weight on purpose.
- Relevance to E5: the examples in Section 5 (Brownian motion, stochastic volatility, theta-logistic, noisy Ricker) vary \(N_x\), not a grid of parameter dimension against observation dimension. The numerical scores in Section 6 were not used below, because they are not a scaling account for E5.
- Relevance to E6: not relevant.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: the expected-squared-jumping-distance trigger in Section 4.1, display of \(\widehat{\mathrm{ESJD}}_{d,r}\); the `novel-esjd` choice of \(N_x\) in Section 4.2, which was read as the discussion's description of a cost-minimising choice among candidate particle counts; the `replace` backward-kernel update, which leaves parameter weights unchanged (Section 6). Sections 5's tables were not transcribed. No numbered theorem was seen; the exactness claim is attributed to Duan and Fulop (2014), which was not opened.
- What the paper does not give us: the kernel-switching rule the 2026 discussion points to, or any result under a biased likelihood.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 filter adapts the ensemble size by a variance threshold (Section 4.1 of that paper) and leaves the exact posterior. This preprint adapts the particle count in order to stay on the exact posterior.

## Rosato, Murphy, Varsi, Horridge and Maskell

- Citation, as printed: Conor Rosato, Joshua Murphy, Alessandro Varsi, Paul Horridge and Simon Maskell, "Enhanced SMC2: Leveraging Gradient Information from Differentiable Particle Filters Within Langevin Proposals", arXiv:2407.17296v1, 24 July 2024. The footer identifies an IEEE copyright and a conference paper.
- Path: `Literature/2407.17296v1.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: whether a Langevin proposal that uses the gradient of a particle-filter log-likelihood, estimated by automatic differentiation under common random numbers, improves parameter estimates inside a distributed SMC2 sampler relative to a random walk.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the gradient is the derivative of a particle-filter log-likelihood (Section II-D and Appendix A), not of an ensemble Kalman likelihood. The inner filter remains a particle filter, with a differentiable resampling step. The Langevin proposal in Section III-D, display (30), has no accept-reject step. The authors note that a single common seed gives a biased gradient (Introduction, citing Arya et al., 2022, which was not opened) and average over seeds. The implementation uses PyTorch. The outer sampler is an SMC sampler with an L-kernel, run for a fixed number of iterations \(K\), rather than the resample-move SMC2 of Chopin et al. as used in Golightly et al. (2026).
- Relevance to E1: not relevant.
- Relevance to E2: not relevant. The proposal is not chosen among delayed-acceptance kernels.
- Relevance to E3: this is the citation in Section 6 of Golightly et al. (2026), and it does not supply the gradient that citation needs. The score in (18) is \(\nabla \log \pi(\theta) = \nabla \log p(\theta) + \nabla \log p(y_{1:T} \mid \theta)\), with the second term estimated from particle weights (Appendix A, displays (42) and (43)). A Metropolis-Hastings correction of a proposal that uses this score would preserve a particle-filter target, not \(\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{enkf}}\). The Langevin proposal itself is used without an accept-reject step (Section III-D). Section V reports, on a linear Gaussian model and on a two-parameter SIR model, higher effective sample size and lower mean squared error than a random walk, over five Monte Carlo runs. Those comparisons are against the authors' random walk, on their targets, and are not a result about the ensemble Kalman posterior.
- Relevance to E4: not relevant.
- Relevance to E5: the reported dimensions are three parameters (linear Gaussian) and two parameters (SIR). There is no observation-dimension grid.
- Relevance to E6: not relevant.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: the decomposition (18); the weight-gradient estimator (42)-(43) in Appendix A; the Langevin proposal (30). No theorem is stated. The numerical values in Tables II and III were read and are not repeated here, because they are not estimates under the 2026 target.
- What the paper does not give us: a derivative of (13), an accept-reject correction aimed at \(\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{enkf}}\), or a linear-noise score.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 resample-move step evaluates an ensemble Kalman likelihood and uses a corrected Metropolis-Hastings kernel. This paper differentiates a particle filter and proposes without a correction.

## Temfack and Wyse

- Citation, as printed: Dhorasso Temfack and Jason Wyse, "Efficient sequential Bayesian inference for state-space epidemic models using ensemble data assimilation", arXiv:2512.05650v4, 18 May 2026.
- Path: `Literature/2512.05650v4.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: whether replacing the inner particle filter of SMC2 by an ensemble Kalman filter, with a state-dependent observation variance and an unbiased Gaussian density estimator, gives usable sequential inference for state-space epidemic models at lower cost than SMC2.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the observation variance is built for Poisson or negative-binomial incidence (Section 2.2, display (10)), not for a general nonlinear observation density. The likelihood in (17) is the Ghurye and Olkin unbiased estimator of a Gaussian density, which is unbiased for that Gaussian and not for the true epidemic likelihood. The mutation is an independent proposal inside particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings (Algorithm 2), not delayed acceptance. The paper's own Section 2.3 states that the resulting posterior still does not target the true posterior, because of the Gaussian assumption, even after the finite-ensemble correction.
- Relevance to E1: not relevant. No neighbour search.
- Relevance to E2: not relevant. The kernel is not chosen adaptively among screens.
- Relevance to E3: not relevant. No gradient proposal.
- Relevance to E4: a constraint on what "unbiased" can mean. Section 2.2, following Drovandi et al. (2022), says that the ordinary Gaussian density formed from the ensemble moments is biased for the idealised Gaussian predictive, and that the Ghurye and Olkin estimator removes that finite-sample bias when \(N_x > d_y + 3\). It does not remove the Gaussian approximation of the forecast. A nearest-neighbour surrogate of either estimator is a further approximation, and this paper does not analyse it.
- Relevance to E5: the examples are a simulated SEIR model and the 2022 United States monkeypox incidence series (Sections 3.1 and 3.2). The discussion (Section 4) says the method suits moderately high-dimensional or computationally constrained epidemic models and that the bias may be worse when the system is strongly nonlinear or multimodal. No \((d_\theta, d_y)\) grid is reported. One numerical comparison was read: in one simulated example, eSMC2 took about 543 seconds on average against about 3766 seconds for SMC2. The monkeypox table was not transcribed.
- Relevance to E6: not relevant. There is no Rao-Blackwellised correction. The observation model is handled by putting a state-dependent variance inside the ensemble Kalman filter itself.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: Algorithm 1, the ensemble Kalman filter with variance (10)-(11) and likelihood (17); Algorithm 3, the outer SMC2 loop; the statement in Section 2.3 that exactness fails because of the Gaussian assumption. The convergence rate \(O(N_x^{-1/2})\) quoted in Section 4 is attributed to Chen et al. (2022), which was not opened, and is stated there for linear Gaussian systems only.
- What the paper does not give us: delayed acceptance, a KD-tree, a gradient move, or a comparison with Golightly et al. (2026). The discussion suggests a nested particle filter with the inner filter replaced by an ensemble Kalman filter, and leaves that as future work. That suggestion was read; it is not an algorithm in the file.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): both nest an ensemble Kalman filter inside an SMC2 recursion and both target an ensemble Kalman posterior rather than the exact posterior. Temfack and Wyse add a state-dependent observation variance and the Ghurye and Olkin correction, mutate with an independent proposal and particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings, and study epidemic incidence. Golightly et al. (2026) use delayed acceptance, a random-walk ensemble MCMC move, and, for nonlinear observations, the Rao-Blackwellised particle filter of their Section 4.3. Neither paper implements the other's extension.

## Papadakis, Mémin, Cuzol and Gengembre

- Citation, as printed: Nicolas Papadakis, Etienne Mémin, Anne Cuzol and Nicolas Gengembre, "Data assimilation with the weighted ensemble Kalman filter", *Tellus* 62A (2010), 673-697.
- Path: `Literature/Tellus A - 2010 - PAPADAKIS - Data assimilation with the weighted ensemble  Kalman filter.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: whether an ensemble Kalman shift can be used as the proposal inside a particle filter so that the update still reweights, and whether that hybrid is accurate with a small ensemble on high-dimensional geophysical models.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the object is the filtering distribution of the state, not a posterior for a static parameter. The weight (44) in Section 3.3 contains the transition density \(p(x_k^{(i)} \mid x_{k-1}^{(i)})\). Section 3.3 states that this density is usually unavailable when the dynamical time step is finer than the observation interval. The proposal covariance treats the forecast covariance as a mean-field quantity, which the authors say is only exact as the ensemble size tends to infinity (Section 3.1).
- Relevance to E1, E2, E3, E4, E5: not relevant to those extensions. The paper does not treat parameter particles, neighbour searches, surrogate weights, or a scaling grid in \((d_\theta, d_y)\).
- Relevance to E6: this is the contrast drawn in Section 4.3 of Golightly et al. (2026). The weighted ensemble Kalman filter proposes with an ensemble Kalman shift and corrects with the weight (44), which still requires the transition density. The 2026 Rao-Blackwellised weight (24) cancels the forecast density and does not evaluate the transition, which is what lets that filter integrate a fine state grid out of the weight. The two corrections are not the same, and this paper does not discuss a static-parameter target.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: Algorithm 1 in Section 3.3, and the weight (44). Sections 4 to 6, the numerical experiments, were not read in detail. The abstract's claim of efficiency on geophysical examples was read; the tables were not.
- What the paper does not give us: a parameter posterior, a likelihood estimator for \(\theta\), or a Rao-Blackwellised weight that avoids the transition density.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 Rao-Blackwellised filter is a parameter filter whose inner weight marginalises the latent path. The weighted ensemble Kalman filter is a state filter whose weight keeps that path.

## Katzfuss, Stroud and Wikle (2016)

- Citation, as printed: Matthias Katzfuss, Jonathan R. Stroud and Christopher K. Wikle, "Understanding the Ensemble Kalman Filter", *The American Statistician* 70 (2016), 350-357.
- Path: `Literature/Understanding the Ensemble Kalman Filter.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: how the stochastic ensemble Kalman filter relates to the Kalman filter, in notation familiar to statisticians, and which operational variants are in use.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the derivation in Section 3 assumes a linear Gaussian state-space model. The authors state that as the ensemble size tends to infinity the filter converges to the Kalman filter in that case, and that a nonlinear or non-Gaussian model makes the shift an approximation (Sections 3.3 and 4.5). Parameter estimation is either state augmentation with artificial evolution noise, which assumes a joint linear Gaussian model and can fail for covariance parameters (Section 4.4, citing Stroud and Bengtsson, 2007, not opened), or an approximate likelihood from the filter. There is no SMC2 recursion.
- Relevance to E1, E2, E3, E4: not relevant. No tree, no kernel choice, no Langevin proposal, and no surrogate weight.
- Relevance to E5: Section 1 states that the filter is used in data assimilation with tens of millions of dimensions, and Section 2 that state and observation dimensions of \(10^7\) and \(10^5\) arise in weather prediction. Those are state dimensions under a known parameter, not a grid of static-parameter dimension against observation dimension for a nested filter. Section 4.4 says likelihood-based parameter estimates have succeeded when the number of parameters is relatively small, and that high-dimensional parameters remain open. The 2019 review is not this file.
- Relevance to E6: Section 4.5 lists hybrid particle-filter ensemble Kalman filters as one response when the Gaussian shift is not satisfactory, citing papers that were not opened. It does not compare a Rao-Blackwellised correction with a plain ensemble Kalman likelihood.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: Algorithm 1, the stochastic ensemble Kalman filter, in Section 3.3; the gain estimator (11) in Section 3.1. The spatial example in Section 4.1 was not used. The paper was read from Section 1 through Section 5, excluding the figure-only detail of the spatial example.
- What the paper does not give us: the 2019 review, a nested parameter filter, or a likelihood formula with a finite-sample bias correction.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): this is an introduction to the inner filter. The 2026 paper uses that filter as a likelihood estimator inside an outer SMC2 recursion.

## Drovandi, Everitt, Golightly and Prangle

- Citation, as printed: Christopher Drovandi, Richard G. Everitt, Andrew Golightly and Dennis Prangle, "Ensemble MCMC: Accelerating Pseudo-Marginal MCMC for State Space Models using the Ensemble Kalman Filter", *Bayesian Analysis* (2022) 17, Number 1, 223-260.
- Path: `Literature/ensemble.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: whether a pseudo-marginal MCMC scheme that replaces the bootstrap particle filter by an ensemble Kalman likelihood can reduce cost on nonlinear state-space models while keeping a usable approximation to the parameter posterior.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the algorithm is one Markov chain, run on a fixed data set. It is not sequential. The target \(p^N_{\mathrm{enkf}}(\theta \mid y) \propto \hat{L}^N_{\mathrm{enkf}}(\theta)\, p(\theta)\) is not the exact posterior, because the ensemble Kalman likelihood is biased (Section 3, after Algorithm 3). Even when the forecast is exactly Gaussian, the plug-in Gaussian density is biased for the idealised Gaussian predictive, so the finite-\(N\) target is not the idealised target \(p^\infty_{\mathrm{enkf}}\). The unbiased estimator in Section 3.3 removes that second bias only when the forecast is Gaussian and \(N > d + 3\).
- Relevance to E1: the discussion (Section 5) suggests the ensemble Kalman likelihood as the cheap stage of a delayed-acceptance MCMC algorithm, citing Sherlock et al. (2017). It does not build a KD-tree or analyse neighbour error.
- Relevance to E2: not relevant. There is no adaptive choice of kernel inside SMC2. Early rejection is discussed as an extension in Section 3.4; that subsection was not read line by line, and it is not E2.
- Relevance to E3: not relevant. No gradient of the ensemble Kalman likelihood is derived.
- Relevance to E4: Section 3.3 is the closest result in this folder to a bias decomposition, and it decomposes a different error. The Ghurye and Olkin estimator is unbiased for the Gaussian density that has the true forecast mean and covariance, given an independent sample from that Gaussian. It is not a bound on the distance between a nearest-neighbour surrogate and the ensemble Kalman posterior, and it is not a bound on the distance between the ensemble Kalman posterior and the exact posterior.
- Relevance to E5: Section 4.3 runs the method on a stochastic Lorenz-96 system with state dimension 50, parameters \((\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3, \sigma)\), observation variance 25, and reports that particle MCMC was not feasible at that dimension. The tuning rule they borrowed from the pseudo-marginal literature suggested 5,000 ensemble members; one chain of 10,000 iterations had acceptance rate 18% and effective sample size 321. That is one high-dimensional state example, not a grid. Section 5 says the method is a middle ground between exact-approximate particle MCMC and data assimilation, and that it relies on a Gaussian approximation of the filtering distribution.
- Relevance to E6: not relevant. Nonlinear observations are mentioned as a possible extension through Katzfuss et al. (2019), which was not opened. There is no Rao-Blackwellised weight.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: Algorithm 3 and the likelihood (8) in Section 3; the statement that the target depends on \(N\) even under a Gaussian forecast; the unbiased Gaussian density estimator in Section 3.3, displayed from Ghurye and Olkin (1969). Sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.4, 4.5 and 4.6 were not transcribed. Section 4.3 and Section 5 were read. The supplement was not read. The discussion's suggestion to put the scheme inside SMC2 (final part of Section 5) is a remark, not an algorithm; the authors leave it as further research.
- What the paper does not give us: a sequential filter, a delayed-acceptance implementation, or a theorem bounding the ensemble Kalman posterior.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): one rejuvenation step of the 2026 filter is one iteration of this scheme. The 2026 filter repeats that step inside SMC2 as new observations arrive, instead of discarding the chain and restarting.

## What constrains each extension

E1 is constrained by Sherlock, Golightly and Henderson, Sections 2 and 3. The tree cost they state is \(O(d \log n)\) search and insert for a balanced tree, in moderate dimension, with a warning about correlation and axis-aligned splits. Their Theorem 1 keeps the expensive target invariant when the second stage evaluates that target and adaptation diminishes. It does not say that a different neighbour set leaves the same kernel. At the dimensions used in that paper (3 to 12) a list can still be the cheaper search when the number of stored points is a few thousand.

E2 is constrained by Chopin, Jacob and Papaspiliopoulos, Section 3.2, by Botha, Kohn, South and Drovandi on particle counts, and by the two files added below: Fearnhead and Taylor, and Botha et al. on kernel switching. Fearnhead and Taylor choose among kernels that are each invariant for the current target, and they treat the tuning as an auxiliary variable. Botha et al. switch between particle marginal Metropolis–Hastings and particle Gibbs, which needs a conditional particle filter. Both invariance arguments use an unbiased particle likelihood. They do not transfer to an ensemble Kalman weight without a fresh argument.

E3 is constrained by Rosato et al., Sections II-D, III-D and Appendix A, and by Chen, Sanz-Alonso and Willett, which changes the gap. Rosato et al. differentiate a particle filter and propose without an accept-reject step. Chen et al. differentiate the ensemble Kalman log-likelihood itself. Their Theorem 3.2 is for linear Gaussian transitions and a Gaussian initial law, and their Algorithm 4.1 is gradient ascent, not a corrected Metropolis–Hastings kernel. Lowe, Golightly and Sherlock, Section 4.1, differentiate a linear-noise posterior, and their second stage is an unbiased particle likelihood.

E4 is constrained by Chopin et al., Proposition 1, by Sherlock, Golightly and Henderson, Section 5.1, by Drovandi et al., Section 3.3, and by Bon, Lee and Drovandi, which changes the gap. Bon et al. put a surrogate inside the mutation of an SMC sampler and keep the expensive likelihood as the target of the second stage. They do not replace the incremental weight by the surrogate. No file bounds the distance between a nearest-neighbour weight and the ensemble Kalman posterior.

E5 is constrained by Katzfuss, Stroud and Wikle (2016), Sections 1 and 4.4, by their 2018 preprint of the hierarchical review (Proposition 2), and by Drovandi et al., Section 4.3. Proposition 2 gives \(\mathrm{var}(\log L^E) = O(n/N)\) in one independent linear example, when the forecast members are drawn independently from the true forecast. That is the scaling the 2026 paper points to. It is not a grid of static-parameter dimension against observation dimension for a nested filter.

E6 is constrained by Papadakis et al., Section 3.3, and by the absence of a decision rule in the other papers. The weighted ensemble Kalman filter corrects an ensemble Kalman proposal with a weight that still contains the transition density. Drovandi et al. and Temfack and Wyse stay with the ensemble Kalman likelihood and do not add the 2026 Rao-Blackwellised weight. No file proves when that correction is worth its cost. When the observation density used in the ensemble Kalman filter equals the true observation density, the ratio in the 2026 weight (24) is identically one, so the correction does not change the target.

## Katzfuss, Stroud and Wikle (preprint of the hierarchical review)

- Citation, as printed: Matthias Katzfuss, Jonathan R. Stroud and Christopher K. Wikle, "Ensemble Kalman methods for high-dimensional hierarchical dynamic space-time models", arXiv:1704.06988v2, 8 August 2018. The research plan identifies the journal version as Katzfuss, Stroud and Wikle (2019), later cited as the 2020 *Journal of the American Statistical Association* paper. That journal file was not the file read here.
- Path: `Literature/Katzfuss2017-ensemble-kalman-methods.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: how to combine ensemble Kalman updates with statistical filtering and smoothing so that high-dimensional nonlinear, non-Gaussian, spatio-temporal state-space models still have a usable joint posterior for states and parameters.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the particle ensemble Kalman filter in Algorithm 4 moves the parameter with a proposal \(q_t(\theta_t \mid \theta_{t-1})\), and the numerical example uses artificial evolution. The weight is an integrated ensemble Kalman likelihood, not an unbiased particle likelihood. Forecast members in Proposition 2 are independent draws from the true forecast, which an interacting ensemble does not supply.
- Relevance to E1, E2, E3, E4: not relevant. No tree, no choice among delayed-acceptance kernels, no gradient of the likelihood, and no nearest-neighbour weight.
- Relevance to E5: this is the review the 2026 paper points to. Proposition 2, in the setting of their Example 5, states that the variance of the log ensemble Kalman likelihood is \(O(n/N)\) as \(n, N \to \infty\), with the variance over independent forecast draws from the true forecast. A tridiagonal forecast covariance produced a plot similar to their Figure 2(d); that similarity is a simulation, not a second theorem. If the forecast is non-Gaussian, the ensemble Kalman likelihood converges to the Dawid–Sebastiani score. The particle ensemble Kalman filter is described as working best when the number of unknown parameters is not too high (Section 4.1.2). Section 5's numerical examples were not transcribed.
- Relevance to E6: not relevant. Nonlinear observations are handled by an integrated likelihood or a Laplace approximation inside the ensemble update, not by the 2026 weight (24).
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: the ensemble Kalman likelihood (16) in Section 3.2.2; Proposition 2; Algorithm 4. The proof of Proposition 2 was read. Sections 4.2 and 5 were not transcribed.
- What the paper does not give us: a static-parameter SMC2 recursion, a delayed-acceptance screen, or a measured region in \((d_\theta, d_y)\) for the 2026 filter.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 particle ensemble Kalman filter (their Algorithm 5) adapts this construction, and the 2026 nested filter replaces the artificial evolution by a resample-move on a static parameter.

## Fearnhead and Taylor

- Citation, as printed: Paul Fearnhead and Benjamin M. Taylor, "An Adaptive Sequential Monte Carlo Sampler", arXiv:1005.1193v2. The header prints 10 May 2010. The research plan identifies the journal version as Fearnhead and Taylor (2013), *Bayesian Analysis*. That journal file was not read.
- Path: `Literature/Fearnhead2013-adaptive-smc.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: how an SMC sampler can choose among Markov kernels and learn a scaling, using the expected squared jumping distance of the particles.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: each candidate kernel is assumed invariant for the current target \(\pi_t\). The likelihood that defines \(\pi_t\) is the target density of the SMC sampler, not a random ensemble Kalman likelihood. The key modelling assumption, stated in the introduction, is that a good kernel at time \(t\) remains a good kernel at later times. Theorem 4.1 needs the sequence of utility functions to converge.
- Relevance to E1: not relevant.
- Relevance to E2: this is a source for a choice among invariant kernels. Section 3 treats the tuning parameter as an auxiliary random variable. The proposal may depend on quantities calculated from the current particles, such as a variance estimate, and that dependence is suppressed in the notation. The selection criterion is the expected squared jumping distance. The paper does not treat a biased likelihood, and it does not choose between a delayed-acceptance kernel and a plain Metropolis–Hastings kernel inside SMC2.
- Relevance to E3, E4, E5, E6: not relevant. No filter gradient, no surrogate weight, and no \((d_\theta, d_y)\) grid. Section 5's numerical comparisons were not transcribed.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: the auxiliary-variable construction in Section 3; the expected-squared-jumping-distance criterion; Theorem 4.1, whose statement was read and whose proof was not checked line by line.
- What the paper does not give us: an invariance proof that survives replacement of an unbiased likelihood by an ensemble Kalman likelihood.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 move is one fixed random-walk kernel, optionally delayed acceptance with a fixed neighbour count. This paper adapts the kernel. The adaptation can be reused only for kernels that each preserve the ensemble Kalman target.

## Botha, Kohn, South and Drovandi (kernel switching)

- Citation, as printed: Imke Botha, Robert Kohn, Leah South and Christopher Drovandi, "Adaptively switching between a particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings and a particle Gibbs kernel in SMC2", arXiv:2307.11553v1, 21 July 2023. The identifier in the scoping note is this arXiv number. It was confirmed from the file header before citing it.
- Path: `Literature/Botha2023-switching-kernels-smc2.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: whether SMC2 can switch, at each mutation, between a particle marginal Metropolis–Hastings kernel and a particle Gibbs kernel, using a score that divides a jumping distance by the number of state particles.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: both kernels use an unbiased particle-filter estimator or a conditional particle filter (Sections 2 and 3). Particle Gibbs needs an invariant path from the conditional particle filter. An ensemble Kalman filter does not supply that path. The reweighting step always uses the target of the default kernel, even when the alternate kernel mutates the particles (Section 4.2).
- Relevance to E1: not relevant.
- Relevance to E2: a constraint, and a score that can be adapted only after the invariance assumptions are dropped. The score of a kernel is the minimum, over parameters, of the squared jumping distance, divided by the number of state particles used by that kernel (Section 4.2, after display (12)). The switch is between two exact kernels. It is not a switch between delayed-acceptance kernels with different neighbour counts.
- Relevance to E3, E4, E5, E6: not relevant. Section 5's experiments were not transcribed.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: the squared jumping distance (11) and the per-parameter version (12); the score \(\min(\widehat{\mathrm{SJD}})/N_x\). No numbered invariance theorem was seen; exactness is attributed to the particle marginal Metropolis–Hastings and particle Gibbs kernels of Andrieu, Doucet and Holenstein (2010), which was not opened.
- What the paper does not give us: a kernel switch that an ensemble Kalman weight can use unchanged.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 discussion cites this line of work for choosing among kernels. The file chooses between two particle-MCMC kernels on the exact extended target.

## Christen and Fox

- Citation, as printed: J. Andrés Christen and Colin Fox, "MCMC using an Approximation". The file is an author preprint. It does not print a year. The research plan identifies the paper as Christen and Fox (2005). That year was not read off the file. `pdfinfo` reports 15 pages.
- Path: `Literature/Christen2005-mcmc-using-approximation.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: whether a Metropolis–Hastings kernel that screens proposals with a cheap approximation, and then corrects with the expensive target, still has that target as an invariant distribution.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the chain is a single Markov chain on a fixed target \(f\), not an SMC2 cloud. Theorem 1 requires the parent Metropolis–Hastings kernel to be \(f\)-irreducible, the proposal \(q\) to be reversible, and \(q(y \mid x) > 0\) to imply that the approximation is positive at \(y\). If the second stage evaluates an ensemble Kalman likelihood rather than the exact posterior, the invariant distribution is the ensemble Kalman target, not \(\pi(\theta \mid y)\).
- Relevance to E1: the theorem is the invariance result that a neighbour screen needs. It does not discuss trees or approximate neighbours. A different but positive approximation changes the kernel and, provided the second stage still evaluates \(f\), leaves \(f\) invariant.
- Relevance to E2: a kernel that uses one fixed approximation preserves \(f\) under Theorem 1. A choice among such kernels is not in this file.
- Relevance to E3: not relevant.
- Relevance to E4: a constraint. The second stage is what restores \(f\). Using the approximation as the only acceptance probability is not the algorithm Theorem 1 analyses.
- Relevance to E5 and E6: not relevant. The imaging examples after Theorem 2 were not transcribed.
- Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: Theorem 1, which was read with its proof.
- What the paper does not give us: an SMC implementation, or a result for a biased likelihood used as the final target.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 stage-two ratio (20) is this correction, with the ensemble Kalman likelihood in the role of \(f\).

## Sherlock, Thiery and Golightly

The research plan identifies the journal version as Sherlock, Thiery and Golightly (2021), *Annals of Statistics*. That journal file was not read. The stored file includes supplementary proofs; those proofs were not read line by line.
- Path: `Literature/Sherlock2021-delayed-acceptance-efficiency.pdf`.
- Question the paper answers: how the efficiency of an optimally tuned delayed-acceptance random walk, relative to the optimally tuned parent random walk, depends on the cost and the accuracy of the cheap approximation.
- Assumptions that would fail for a nested ensemble Kalman filter: the analysis is a high-dimensional diffusion limit for random-walk Metropolis and for a pseudo-marginal version whose second stage uses unbiased estimates (introduction, the particle-filter and panel-data examples). - Algorithms, estimators, or theorems a later derivation might reuse: the two-parameter efficiency description in the introduction; Theorem 5.1 on the efficiency surface of the delayed-acceptance pseudo-marginal random walk.
- What the paper does not give us: a finite-sample rule for SMC2, or a result under a biased second stage.
- How it differs from Golightly et al. (2026): the 2026 screen is this delayed-acceptance idea inside one rejuvenation sweep, with a nearest-neighbour surrogate whose cost is the neighbour search rather than a deterministic approximation of a kinetic model.
