\section{Diagnostic}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

Equation (12) of \citet{whitaker2025} freezes the hazard at the left endpoint of the remaining interval. The mean of the remaining incidence is that frozen hazard times the remaining time. \citet[Section 3.1]{golightly2015} use the same constant-hazard Gaussian step for remaining reaction counts. \citet[Section 3.2]{golightly2019} say that this step is often unreasonable, and their Section 6 says that accuracy falls as the inter-observation time grows. \citet[p.~25]{whitaker2025} call linearity of the latent process, over an interval no longer than the observation interval, a further assumption in the derivation of the conditioned hazard. Their Section 3.4 does not use the word linear, and it does not print a second condition beyond equation (12) and the display after (18). We do not separate those two passages. The constant-hazard step is the assumption the limitations paragraph is pointing at.

The same paragraph says the conditioned hazard is likely to propose trajectories inconsistent with the true conditioned process, when cumulative incidence does not evolve linearly. The law named on the page is the true conditioned process. The sentence is about the trajectories the hazard proposes. It does not say that the discretised transition in the numerator of Equation~\eqref{eq:weight} is the wrong model, and it does not say that the weight targets a different distribution.

A poor \(h^{*}\) changes \(q\) and therefore the weights. On the printed ratio, the importance target of one propagate step stays the discretised transition times the observation mass function. This is a reading of the printed weight. It is not a proof that the particle filter converges.

In that sense, inconsistent is a property of the proposal. No paper we opened states a numerical spacing at which the 2025 guide fails for this epidemic model. \citet[Section 5.1]{golightly2019} report that a linear form fails to describe exponential decay, and that effective sample size falls as the interval length grows, for a death process. We do not copy those figures. They are not results for the epidemic model.

The observable is the effective sample size of the unnormalised incidence weights in one window,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ess}
\mathrm{ESS}(\tilde w_{1:N})
= \frac{\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde w_i\bigr)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde w_i^2},
\end{equation}
as printed by \citet[Section 5.2]{golightly2019}. Zero weights are included. If every weight in a window is zero, Equation~\eqref{eq:ess} is undefined and the recorded value is 0, as a convention for the table. A draw has weight zero when the correction in Equation~\eqref{eq:hstar} is undefined, when a component of the hazard passed to the Poisson draw is negative, or when the updated counts would be negative. \citet{whitaker2025} do not print a truncation. \citet[Section 3.2]{golightly2019} truncate their equation (5) to keep it positive. We do not copy that truncation onto the 2025 display.

The prediction, fixed before the run, is that the median of this effective sample size across replicate data sets is smaller at the coarsest pre-registered spacing than at the published unit spacing. A myopic proposal, with \(h^{*}\) replaced by \(h\), is a comparator. It is not a second target. The diagnostic will appear as this comparison. It is not a threshold, and it does not say that the filtering distribution changes.

Several claims are outside the paper. Section~\ref{sec:residual} states a different proposal. We do not replace \(q\) by equation (19) of \citet{golightly2019} or by Theorem 4.2 of \citet{corstanje2023}. We do not drop \(h(0, x_0)\) from their ratio (16). We do not present a truncated negative hazard as the 2025 guide. We do not claim a spacing, beyond the three values fixed before the run, at which the guide fails. We do not claim that a poor \(h^{*}\) changes the filtering target. We do not use the death-model table of \citet{golightly2019} as evidence about the epidemic model. We do not compute a gold-standard posterior.
