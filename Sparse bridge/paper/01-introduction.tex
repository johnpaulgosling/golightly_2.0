\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Cumulative incidence is a common observation in epidemic modelling when infection times are hidden and only counts of new cases are recorded in successive windows. \citet{whitaker2025} take a time-discretised susceptible-exposed-infected-removed model, and its susceptible-infected-removed reduction, as the latent transmission process, and they assimilate noisy incidence with a particle filter. The same construction matters more widely for partially observed jump processes, where a conditioned proposal is used because forward simulation ignores the next observation \citep{golightly2015,golightly2019}.

The inferential obstacle is an intractable observed-data likelihood. The data are incomplete and discrete, the reporting model is noisy, and the latent incidence between two observation times is a path of counts. A bootstrap filter would draw that path from the unconditioned transition and weight by the observation alone. \citet[p.~8]{whitaker2025} give two reasons not to stop there. Resampling static parameters collapses them, and forward simulation of incidence ignores the next count.

\citet{whitaker2025} meet the second difficulty with a conditioned hazard. Particles are propagated by replacing the Poisson rate with a hazard derived from a Gaussian approximation of the cumulative incidence between the current time and the next observation, given that observation. The particle is then reweighted by the discretised transition and the observation mass function. The limitations section of that paper states that a further assumption in the derivation is linearity of the latent process over an interval no longer than the observation interval. When the epidemic is sparsely observed, so that cumulative incidence does not evolve linearly, ``the conditioned hazard is likely to result in trajectories that are inconsistent with the true conditioned process'' \citep[p.~25]{whitaker2025}.

That paragraph does not give a numerical spacing at which the failure begins, and it does not say that the importance weight then targets a different distribution. It names recent work on sampling conditioned jump processes, in particular \citet{golightly2019} and \citet{corstanje2023}, as relevant and open to adaptation. It does not print either algorithm as a replacement inside the filter.

The contribution is in four parts. Firstly, we read the constant-hazard step in the 2025 derivation as the linearity assumption named on that page, and we read the inconsistency as a property of the proposal. Secondly, we report the effective sample size of the incidence weights on the synthetic susceptible-infected-removed mechanism of \citet{whitaker2025}, at the published spacing and at two coarser spacings fixed in advance. Thirdly, we record why equation (19) of \citet{golightly2019} and Theorem 4.2 of \citet{corstanje2023} do not replace the proposal as the filter is written. Fourthly, we replace the Poisson means by the increment of a restarted incidence linear-noise path plus a conditional residual mean, and we compare the effective sample size of that proposal with the linear guide on the same mechanism. The density in the denominator remains a product of Poisson probabilities for those incidence increments.

The illustration is synthetic. \citet{whitaker2025} also discuss weekly Ebola counts and weekly COVID-19 counts. Those counts are plotted and are not stored here as a data file, so we do not reanalyse them.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} compares the guides that were read. Section~\ref{sec:filter} restates the 2025 filter and the linear incidence guide. Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic} states what the diagnostic claims. Section~\ref{sec:residual} states the residual proposal. Section~\ref{sec:simulation} reports both comparisons. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} records the qualifications.
